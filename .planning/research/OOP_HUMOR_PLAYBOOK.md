# OOP Humor Playbook — generating in Nikhil's taste, inside this game's voice

_Run 10 deliverable. Companion to `OOP_HUMOR_CATALOG.md`. This is the brief a later humor pass (Run 05) reads INSTEAD of raw OOP examples._

## The one-paragraph thesis

Out-of-Pocket's humor has three useful layers: **targets** (rules, payers, bureaucracy, himself — never patients), **materials** (real artifacts, real regulations, real absurd endpoints, explained accurately before being mocked), and **delivery** (captions, cappers, escalating bits, speech). All three can inform PrivacyQuest. The governing distinction is not "written surfaces may be witty; characters may not." It is **comic premise versus joke announcement**. The funny thing should be a recognizable healthcare behavior, a human motive, a status reversal, or the absurd practical work of complying with HIPAA. A character may be funny and may speak in complete, clever sentences. They simply should not sound as though they have stepped outside the situation to present the audience with the scene's designated joke.

Situations, artifacts, silence, dialogue, item descriptions, citation cards, loading screens, animation, sound, and ordinary room props are all available channels. Choose the one natural to the moment. Authored surfaces are especially good at holding real documents, statistics, and formal language; dialogue is especially good at revealing desire, embarrassment, fatigue, status, and self-deception. A tiny annotation, animation, sound, or visual gag does not need to carry the scene's central comic premise to be worthwhile. Surface pleasure is part of making a joyful game.

## Build the comic premise, then make the whole room pleasurable

Before writing the line, ask what is actually funny in the scene. Strong premises usually contain at least one of these:

- **A motive:** someone is rushing, avoiding work, trying to leave, pleasing a boss, hiding a mistake, or being much too helpful.
- **A contradiction:** the HIPAA training is open on the same workstation as the exposed chart; the five-minute note has been there for two hours.
- **A status change:** the confident gossip becomes the person who has to complete the incident form.
- **A true absurd endpoint:** a serious privacy control depends on a Post-it staying attached.
- **Recognition:** annual training, password rotation, fax confirmations, full secure bins, shared workstations, and the gap between policy and what people really do.

The main beats benefit from consequential specificity: details that cause the failure, reveal the person, or sharpen the contradiction. But not every detail has to justify itself by advancing the scene. A group chat, coffee order, coworker named Kevin, sticky note, tiny sound, or cute notification can add pleasure, rhythm, recognition, or a thread that pays off elsewhere. The standard is intentionality, not utility.

The problem with scattered garnish is not that it is garnish. It is that the details feel interchangeable and do not accumulate into a particular hospital, culture, or cast. Prefer density with memory: recurring names, objects that change over time, props that react differently on repeat interaction, local feuds, staff habits, visual callbacks, and room-specific sounds. A water cooler may deliver no HIPAA lesson and still earn its place by being satisfying to use, carrying a small joke, revealing who works nearby, or continuing an inside thread.

## Guiding principle: intentional joy density

The hospital should reward attention almost everywhere. The aspiration is not merely that each room contains several things; it is that nearly every visible thing feels considered. A jukebox, water cooler, copier, chair, plant, cart, clock, and bulletin board can each contribute through one or more of these:

- a satisfying animation, sound, or tactile response;
- a joke, odd detail, or authored observation;
- a recurring thread or callback that develops across rooms;
- evidence of a specific employee, department, or hospital history;
- a small choice, secret, variation, or repeat-interaction payoff;
- beauty, warmth, atmosphere, or a moment of rest;
- the real texture of healthcare work, including annual training and the gap between policy and practice.

Not every prop needs text and not every interaction needs a punchline. Repetition, restraint, and quiet objects give the richer moments shape. But avoid anonymous filler: when an object is prominent enough for the player to notice or approach, make a deliberate decision about the feeling it contributes. Humor is not a thin rewrite layer applied to quiz dialogue; it is part of the room design, animation, sound, interaction model, and sense that people have lived here.

## The 7 devices that survive

### 1. Found-artifact-as-punchline ★ the best transfer
**How it works in OOP:** a screenshot of a true thing (CMS coverage language, a Reddit thread of patients swapping CPAP data, uBiome's internal memo calling sample-rerunning a "growth hack") presented with a flat caption. The artifact indicts itself; he just holds it up.
**Real examples:** the uBiome fraud exhibits (uBiome post); real EHR lab-format screenshots played as found-object horror (Why Are Docs Late).
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** put the artifact IN the room as an inspectable object. Nobody comments on it. The item description is flat and official — the deadpan is structural.
> *Inspectable tray on the IT Office fax machine:*
> **CONFIRMATION — TRANSMISSION OK — 14 PAGES**
> **RECIPIENT: ANTONIO'S PIZZERIA & GRILL**
> *Someone has written "NO" on it in pen. Nothing else.*

This is also the fix for OOP's meme-caption instinct: the game's equivalent of a caption is a sticky note, a pen annotation, a whiteboard correction — diegetic captions.

### 2. Weird-rule deadpan recitation
**How it works in OOP:** recite the actual rule, accurately, with a straight face; the absurdity self-carries (the Birthday Rule, the 2 Midnight Rule, "It is now an office visit").
**Real examples:** Silly Little Rules and More Weird Rules — both posts explain the true mechanism before any joke lands.
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** a character states the real rule from inside the work of applying it. They may be terse or expansive depending on who they are and what they want. The rule's collision with ordinary working life supplies the laugh; fragmentary delivery is an option, not a requirement.
> **JOANNA:** Thirty days. Extendable once. There's a form for the extension.
> *[stamps something]*
> **JOANNA:** There's a form for the form. Anyway.
The last line works only if it belongs to this clerk and this moment. If it can be pasted into any bureaucracy scene, replace it with a behavior or detail specific to the rule being applied. The true rule can carry the scene, but a character is allowed to have wit.

### 3. Absurd literalization — as situation, not sentence
**How it works in OOP:** follow a rule to its dumbest true endpoint (the Sunshine Act putting a sad airport pretzel in a federal database; records access so painful "you'd probably rather be in the hospital").
**Real examples:** the pretzel (Silly Little Rules); disposable CPAP part requiring a doctor visit (Paying for Friends).
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** build the endpoint into the room and let the player find it. The game shows the dumbest true consequence; no one points at it.
> *ER Observation zone: the patient status board has every name covered by a privacy sticky note. One sticky note is on the floor. The scene's choice is what you do about it — the absurdity (a federal privacy regime defeated by adhesive) is never voiced.*

### 4. Self-implicating cautionary tale
**How it works in OOP:** he includes himself in the indictment before mocking anyone ("myself included," the Orgo-midterm fake notes aside, "I don't love that I know this"). It buys the right to be cynical.
**Real examples:** fake email jobs "(myself included)" (Why Don't Companies Say); "I don't love that I know this" (Fringe Behaviors).
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** already sanctioned — GAME_DESIGN_PRINCIPLES' "Cautionary Tale" archetype. The confession should preserve the speaker's dignity and self-interest instead of arcing to a lesson-summary. It can stop early, ramble, or land cleanly according to the character.
> **SAM:** I used to keep mine on a — *[gestures at the monitor edge]* — yeah. So.
> *[citation card, separate UI: "§164.308 — workforce security. Written passwords near workstations are the most common audit finding."]*

### 5. Analogy smuggling
**How it works in OOP:** a mundane analogy carries the whole mechanism (healthcare pricing = a coffee shop where "the cash register is operating on Windows NT"; texting patients = texting someone you're flirting with). The analogy is the Trojan horse for comprehension — his core educational move and the game's, per CLAUDE.md.
**Real examples:** the coffee-shop bit (Why Are Docs Late); flirting = patient engagement (How To Text Patients).
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** the analogy must feel like something this character would reach for in this circumstance. It may be imperfect, or it may be lucid and polished if the speaker plausibly thinks that way. Deliberately botching every analogy is its own writerly mannerism.
> **SAM:** It's like a coat check. You get the ticket, the coat stays — no, wait. The ticket is the... *[beat]* You need the key thing. That's the point. Don't email the key thing.
The example's botch reveals Sam thinking in real time; it is not what makes the analogy permissible. Another character might deliver a clean analogy because explaining this well is part of how they move through the world.

### 6. Data-as-indictment (chart-as-punchline, stripped of the punchline)
**How it works in OOP:** the number does the emotional work — 129.5 tracked hours of care; billing taking 3x the time of care coordination; a $16M fine against a 78M-record breach.
**Real examples:** Laura Stratte's time-tracking charts (Time Toxicity); Anthem fine vs. breach scope (Change Healthcare).
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** the stat appears on an authored surface — citation card, codex entry, break-room poster — stated flat, no editorializing. This is also where the game's licensed editorial POV (IDENTITY_AUDIT: "the game should have opinions") lives safely: the opinion is in the *selection* of the stat, not in commentary on it.
> *Citation card after a breach scenario: "Most reported breaches are not hackers. They are lost, unencrypted laptops."*
> *Break Room poster, printed: "DAYS SINCE LAST MISDIRECTED FAX: 30" — the 30 crossed out in pen, "3" written over it.*
(The poster is device 1 and 6 shaking hands. That crossover — flat data on a defaced official artifact — may be the single most OOP-shaped thing this game can do without anyone speaking.)

### 7. Verdict compression → proportion and reaction
**How it works in OOP:** two-word verdicts ("Very PE-core."), one-word register drops, the refusal to elaborate. The instinct is compression.
**Real examples:** "Very PE-core." (Pixel Tracking); "That shit is great" as the entire verdict on marriage (Marriage post).
**PrivacyQuest transfer:** match the reaction to the size and social reality of the mistake. Sometimes that is one word and silence. Sometimes the funny response is a complete sentence, a procedural consequence, or the character suddenly losing confidence. Compression is useful when it reveals attitude; it is not a house style, and the wit is allowed to survive.
> *Player picks the wrong answer.*
> **NINA:** No.
> *[goes back to typing. Citation card slides in beside the dialogue.]*

## Devices that require restraint

- **Escalating hypothetical** ("Everyone is pro-consumerism until I suggest a marketplace for organs"). This can work in dialogue or choice text when escalation reveals the character's reasoning. It fails when each new noun exists only to advertise that the writer is escalating a bit. Choice options must remain plausible enough to preserve quiz integrity.
- **Nested-acronym mockery as commentary** (MACRA-contains-CHIP, the TEFCA tumbleweed). Characters who actually deal with the acronyms may mock them. Ground the reaction in their job and make the underlying rule intelligible; generic "government acronyms are crazy" material is too interchangeable.
- **Mock-formal register undercut by crude slang** ("Co-Bruh," "deez nutz," "F*** it we ball"). Double ban: it's a joke-writer's whiplash move, and the register is unusable in a training context regardless. Nothing to salvage; the *scary-term-mundane-reveal* half of it survives inside device 2.
- **Pop-culture non-sequitur capper** ("If I die, bury me inside the Gucci Store."). Usually weak here because it abandons the healthcare situation for a detachable reference, not because quotable lines are forbidden. Keep one only when the reference belongs to the character and changes how we understand the moment.
- **Chart with a joke caption.** Keep it when the caption adds a real point of view or exposes what the chart obscures. Cut it when it merely instructs the player to laugh. A diegetic annotation is often natural, but making it handwritten does not automatically make it funny.
- **Fourth-wall breaks.** OOP does them constantly; GAME_DESIGN_PRINCIPLES caps the whole game at one or two. The cap is already spent in design intent — do not add more under an OOP banner.

## Register guardrails (reconciling with VOICE_AND_HUMOR.md)

1. **His targets are the law here too:** rules, systems, vendors, bureaucracy, yourself. Never patients, never suffering. The one patient-gallows post that works (Time Toxicity) works because the patient herself is telling it — in-game, gallows humor may come only from a patient NPC's own mouth about their own situation, and gently.
2. **Accuracy before texture.** OOP recites the real rule correctly before mocking it, every time. Same order here: the citation card is always straight; the humor lives in the room around it.
3. **Cynicism must be answered.** "The fine is smaller than the breach" (Change Healthcare) and "HIPAA enforcement is toothless" are true-flavored and corrosive. Rule: an NPC may voice a cynical take ONLY if the game answers it — through the scenario's outcome, another character, or the finale's verdict logic. Unanswered systemic cynicism is off the table for a training-adjacent game.
4. **No winking fraud endorsement.** Marriage-gaming, "cowboying" your CPAP, "theoretically" framings — usable as *character quirk or villain pitch* (a hustler NPC the player scores against), never as neutral narration or a sympathetic NPC's advice.
5. **Do-not-port list** (from source posts, regardless of funniness): the suicidal-ideation throwaway (Why Are Docs Late), blood-rule mortality gag (More Weird Rules), organ-marketplace and diagnosis-betting bits as anything but villain material (Ethically Dubious), all profanity, "dat ass," innuendo ("nighttime measurements").
6. **Give comedy breathing room.** Not every sentence should compete for attention. Use ordinary, load-bearing speech wherever the scene needs it, but do not enforce a ratio of boring lines, ban multiple funny beats, or flatten a character to satisfy a wit budget.
7. **Verbatim ≠ shippable.** Nothing from the catalog lands in `gameData.json` as-is; generate in-device, then Nikhil green-lights. (Run brief commitment.)

## Later pass: the heart needs the same discipline

Humor is the present focus. The patient-story layer needs a separate pass afterward, using the same premise-first standard. The current weak pattern is generic protection and uplift language: "This is who you protected," "she got help," "he was safe," "lives quietly protected." These lines announce the intended emotion in the same way a garnish line announces the intended joke.

The heart should come from a concrete thing privacy allowed a person to keep, choose, avoid, or do: who learned the diagnosis, who did not; whether someone could seek treatment without explaining it at work; whether they got to tell their family in their own time; whether an exposed record changed an ordinary Tuesday. Patients are the stakes, never the punchline, but they also need to be people rather than proof that the player performed a virtuous act. Review the patient reveals, the `PatientStoryReveal` framing, and the ending together when that pass begins. Do not solve it incidentally during the humor rewrite.
