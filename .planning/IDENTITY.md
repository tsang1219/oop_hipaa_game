# Identity

The North Star for what kind of game this is. Use it to judge feature ideas, art direction, dialogue tone, and scope decisions. If a proposal doesn't move toward this, push back on it — even if it's "useful."

PROJECT.md says *what* we're building and *what's next*. This document says *what it feels like in the player's hands* and *what shelf it sits on*.

## Elevator pitch

> A 16-bit hospital RPG where every interaction snaps. Pokémon-style overworld, EarthBound-style NPCs, tower defense and PHI sorting as encounters. The HIPAA happens to you while you play — you don't sit through it.

## Genre & shelf

**Primary:** Edutainment JRPG, in the lineage of Carmen Sandiego, Oregon Trail, Mario Teaches Typing — but with modern feel design, not 90s edutainment flatness.

**Mechanically adjacent:** Pokémon Gen 2 (overworld + encounter loop), EarthBound (modern setting, deadpan NPCs), Stardew Valley (16-bit polish, daily life rhythm).

**What it is NOT:**
- A compliance LMS module with a game skin
- A simulation of working in a hospital
- A serious-tone training tool
- A roguelike, sim, or open-world game

If something starts pulling toward "LMS module" or "simulation," that's the drift to catch.

## Player fantasy

You are a new hospital employee on your first week. The building is bigger and weirder than you expected. Everyone has opinions. Things keep happening. By the end of the day you've handled a few small disasters, met some people, and somehow learned a lot — but it never felt like school.

The fantasy is **competent rookie at a strange new job**, not "compliance trainee."

## Core feel commitments

These are downstream of the Nintendo Test (CLAUDE.md), restated as identity rules:

1. **Feel-first.** The texture of a single interaction matters more than the breadth of features. A perfectly crunchy tower placement beats three half-juicy mechanics.
2. **JRPG cadence.** Walk → talk → encounter → reward → walk. Predictable rhythm with surprise dressed up inside it.
3. **Deadpan humor over corporate cheer.** EarthBound, not eLearning. NPCs are people having a Tuesday, not narrators.
4. **Pixel art and chip audio, always.** 32px tiles, "Press Start 2P," small punchy sounds. No 3D, no flat vector UI, no sans-serif everywhere.
5. **HIPAA is the setting, not the curriculum.** Topics are taught by *being in a hospital where things happen*, not by lessons attached to scenes.

## Comparables (the "if you like ___" list)

If a player loved any of these, the experience should feel familiar:

- **Pokémon Gold/Silver** — overworld, dialogue cadence, encounter loop
- **EarthBound / Mother 3** — modern setting in 16-bit, dry NPC writing
- **Stardew Valley** — polish, sound design, "one more day" pull
- **Papers, Please** — moral micro-decisions with consequences
- **Hacknet** — teaching real domain skills inside a game frame
- **Carmen Sandiego (DOS-era)** — the original "I learned and didn't notice"

If we ever describe the project and none of these come to mind for the listener, the description is wrong.

## Anti-comparables

Things this should never be confused with:
- Generic edu-game asset flips (math blaster, etc.)
- Walking-sim "interactive training" videos
- Gamified LMS dashboards
- Mobile match-3 with HIPAA flavor text

## The shelf-test

Imagine the itch.io / Steam page. Tags: `pixel art`, `RPG`, `educational`, `top-down`, `tower defense`. The screenshot grid shows: a hallway with NPCs, a dialogue choice, a tower defense board, a PHI sort. The description opens with the elevator pitch above. A player browsing finds it — not an L&D manager browsing a vendor list.

If a feature proposal would make the Steam page feel weirder or more corporate, that's a signal.

## How to use this document

When proposing or reviewing a feature, scene, sprite, or piece of writing, ask:
1. Does it move toward the elevator pitch, or away?
2. Would it look at home in any of the comparables?
3. Does it strengthen the player fantasy (rookie at a strange job) or dilute it?
4. Could it appear on the Steam page screenshot grid without feeling out of place?

A "no" on any of these isn't an automatic kill — but it requires a deliberate "we're doing this anyway, here's why."
